Eleven buses. $11,000.
The questions behind the sale.

Fairfield’s records tell us what one buyer paid. They leave taxpayers with harder questions about repair charges, replacement decisions and how the accepted value was justified.

The major accountability gaps

Can taxpayers follow each bus from accurate identification, through condition and value assessment, to approval, transfer and payment? The supplied records leave material breaks in that trail.

Duplicate identifiers in the valuation sheet

Buses 5 and 11 repeat B658992. Buses 144 and 146 repeat B776032. The District acknowledged both errors. Other asset records identify Bus 11 with VIN ending 8993 and Bus 146 with ending 6034. This is a documented accuracy problem in a spreadsheet supplied as support for disposal decisions—not evidence that the physical buses carried duplicate VINs. Finding 06 and sources.

$1,000 per bus establishes the price—not the justification

The invoice, correspondence and receipt document 11 LKQ buses for $11,000. The reviewed production does not establish a complete vehicle-specific comparison supporting that disposal route and net return. It does not establish whether Doug Palmer physically inspected each bus, used photographs or relied on another assessment. Bus 58 is labeled “good for age” and Bus 68 “average for age,” yet both are designated “SCRAP.” Those labels require an explanation of the condition criteria and disposal decision; they do not prove roadworthiness.

What alternatives were evaluated, who accepted the particular price under what authority, and what assessment supported it? Board disposal approvals exist. The missing connection is the documented economic rationale and price-specific approval trail. “Replaced by a newer bus” does not supply that analysis. Finding 03 · Finding 04 · Finding 11.

Additional reconciliation problems

Seven assets were labeled “Disposed Of” although the District said they remained in use. Their retained status was explained; the asset-record status still needs reconciliation. Separately, the newly supplied Bus 123 report differs from its dated detail by $271.33 and two lines, and includes a $7,927.71 generic entry. These Bus 123 exceptions were newly identified in this review and have not yet received a District response. Finding 05 · Finding 07.

Central assessment: The District produced substantial records, but the production still does not provide a complete, verifiable trail showing how certain repair charges were supported, how disposal values were justified, and how the chosen transactions protected taxpayers’ interests. Repeated, specifically identified requests ended with unresolved documentation gaps—not a complete reconciliation. No quantified loss, intentional concealment or favoritism is established by this review.

Transparency must allow verification

Releasing a large volume of records is not the same as providing a complete, traceable explanation of public spending. The practical test is whether a reader can connect an asset and recorded charge to its supporting invoice, authorization, work performed, adjustments and payment—and connect a disposal decision to condition, value, approval and proceeds.

For the specifically disputed Bus 33 and Bus 68 entries, that chain remains incomplete. Work-order numbers and summary charges were supplied, but the requested underlying itemized repair invoices and supporting financial records have not been located in the production. The final response reports no additional records located and closes the request. The search is closed; those financial questions are not resolved.

The District did supply useful records and resolve some questions. Nevertheless, the missing transaction support, acknowledged identifier errors and incomplete valuation rationale limit independent scrutiny. This is a concrete transparency shortcoming in the produced record, not evidence by itself of intentional concealment. No specific District slogan or public transparency pledge is quoted here without its source.

“Replaced by a newer bus.” That is the reason recorded on the supplied disposal forms. For taxpayers, it is the beginning of the explanation—not the end.

A newer bus may be safer, more reliable or cheaper to operate. An older bus can look respectable in a photograph and still have serious structural or mechanical problems. But the financial case should be traceable: what condition was it in, what would keeping it cost, what alternatives were considered, and who decided the sale terms?

The records reviewed here do not provide a complete answer to that chain of questions.

The price is no longer a mystery. The rationale still needs work.

Invoice 2125 identifies 11 buses and a total sale to LKQ of $11,000. A March 19, 2025 buyer email asks the District to invoice $1,000 each, after a conversation with Doug Palmer. The payment receipt follows in May.

That is evidence of an agreed price. It is not, standing alone, evidence of a tested market value or the best available return. The District’s final response says it located no additional bids, offers, quotations, auction records, pickup records, approvals or related disposal records beyond what it had already supplied.

The most serious repair question is a single line: $23,336.

Bus 33’s history records that amount as “MISC PARTS OUT.” The requester gave the District the exact work-order number and date and asked for itemization, authorization, completion and payment records. The final response says no additional supporting financial records were located for that issue.

A generic label is not enough to independently evaluate a charge of that size. That does not prove the charge was improper. It means the production does not let taxpayers verify it.

Another bus, two work orders, the same parts.

On November 8, 2018, Bus 68 has two work orders with the same $12.11 relay and $824.51 door assembly. That is $836.62 twice. Two legitimate repairs, a reposting, or a duplicate? The records supplied do not settle it. Calling it a proven double payment would go beyond the evidence. Leaving it unreconciled is still a legitimate accountability concern.

The paperwork itself needs explaining.

The valuation spreadsheet repeats VIN suffixes for two pairs of buses—errors the District acknowledged. It labels one SCRAP bus good for age and another average for age. Its classifications do not explain the threshold for scrapping. The bus asset listing marks seven vehicles “Disposed Of” even though the District later said they remained on its property and in use.

And the final Bus 123 attachment adds a new exception: 118 visible dated entries totaling $29,342.09, but a report total of $29,613.42 and 120 lines. The $271.33 difference needs reconciliation. A separate $7,927.71 entry has only a generic transportation description. Those new questions have not yet received a District response.

What the photos can—and cannot—tell us

The supplied bus photographs belong in the public record. Viewers can see the visible condition for themselves. They cannot inspect an engine, frame, brakes, or remaining service life from those images. The fair question is not “does it look shiny enough to keep?” It is “where is the dated assessment that connects this vehicle’s condition to the financial decision?”

This was not one vague request.

The requester followed up with asset tags, VINs, named invoices, exact work orders and specific categories of existing records. The District did provide meaningful answers: the LKQ invoice, clarified bus identities, the retained status of seven buses and extensive maintenance histories. Those answers belong in the story.

But closing a records request does not make the unresolved financial questions disappear.

My assessment: Taxpayers have a solid basis to demand a clearer, documented account of these decisions. “We replaced it” and “this is what the buyer paid” do not, by themselves, establish good stewardship. The unresolved repair support and inconsistent asset information deserve specific answers. What we do not yet have is a defensible total of wasted money—or evidence proving fraud, favoritism or deliberate concealment.

Read the source records. Read the District’s responses. Then ask whether the remaining documentation is sufficient to evaluate the use and disposal of public assets.
